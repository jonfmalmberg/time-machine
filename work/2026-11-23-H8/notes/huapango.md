# huapango: Huapango (son huasteco)

## (a) Confidence

Medium confidence in the songs and the definition, low in the size. All 63 claims are `found` by the checker. Every track and album was read from a raw JSON page (iTunes lookup API, MusicBrainz web service), and the definition rests on English and Spanish Wikipedia (Huapango, Son huasteco): the trio of violin, jarana huasteca and huapanguera, the mixed duple/triple rhythm, falsetto duet singing with the violin silent under the voice, the virtuoso violin, improvised coplas, and the dates. The window has three dated layers. English Wikipedia's infobox says "18th century Sierra Huasteca" (this fits the tree's 1750 start), its body text says "end of the 19th century", and Spanish Wikipedia says the trio took its present form with the jarana only in the 1950s. The earliest album verified is from 1966. Most songs are tied to the genre by an album title that names it: Mexican Sones Huastecos, Sones de La Huasteca, Huapangos y Sones Huastecos, Son Huasteco, El Auténtico Son Huasteco, Sones huastecos, El Gusto - 40 Años de Son Huasteco, and Danses et huapangos. The weak ties are as follows:
- Trío Chicontepec rests on album titles that name only the region ("La Huasteca Canta") or the Huastecan violin ("Mexique, l'art du violón husteque", with the page's misspelling and a 1976 date on a 2025 reissue). For El Querreque and El Hidalguense, Spanish Wikipedia also calls them among the most popular huapangos.
- Los Brujos de Huejutla's El Ausente sits on a mixed son jarocho/son huasteco festival album. They are tied by Spanish Wikipedia naming them as son huasteco trovadores.
- The norteño near-miss (Los Reyes De La Farra) is tied to norteño only by its album title, "Para Bailar 20 Huapangos Norteños".
- El Pastor's year (1984) is that of a compilation of original versions, not the original recording.

Titles keep the page's case. The size estimate is a judgement made from catalogue sizes, not a count from any source.

## (c) Neighbours with no id in the tree

- Huapango arribeño (son arribeño) from the Sierra Gorda and Xichú, including Guillermo Velázquez y Los Leones de la Sierra de Xichú: four-piece décima improvisation. It is its own style and should not be folded into huapango.
- Son costumbre and xochisones: the wordless ritual violin and huapanguera music of the Téenek and Nahua Huasteca.
- Huapango as a concert-music theme (Moncayo's Huapango, 1941).
- (Son jarocho, mariachi and norteño do have ids and are used as near targets.)

## (d) What fell short

- No recording before 1966 was verified. Early 78 rpm and LP son huasteco (trios of the 1940s and 1950s) is missing.
- The Smithsonian Folkways liner-note PDF (<https://folkways-media.si.edu/docs/folkways/artwork/SFW40512.pdf>) loads, but its text is bad OCR ("rhe trio" for "the trio"), so no quote was taken. A clean copy would back the trio's history (Los Cantores de la Sierra, Los Caimanes) and the definition.
- Los Brujos de Huejutla's own albums, Los Cantores de la Sierra beyond one anthology track, and "Dueto Xoxocapa" were not found on MusicBrainz or iTunes under those names.
- There is no page-backed original year for Miguel Aceves Mejía's El Pastor.
- La Petenera appears three times by different trios (Elpidio, Xoxocapa, Tamazunchale), and Cielito lindo, El gusto and La huasanga appear twice each. This is deliberate, because the genre is a shared repertoire, but the lead may want to thin the duplicates.
