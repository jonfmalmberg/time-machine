# Where the project came from, and what it is

**Dated record, written 23 November 2026. Its text is not rewritten; where something in it is later found wrong, a dated correction is added inline.** The direction record of the same date ([`2026-11-23-direction.md`](2026-11-23-direction.md)) says where the project is going. This one says where it came from: the intent behind the app as it formed between July and November 2026, before the first release, and Claude's understanding of what the app is as a result.

**How far to trust it.** It is assembled from the project's saved notes, the research plans and results, and a partial re-reading of the original conversation (the early part in full, the middle by sampling). Quotations are Jon's words. Everything else is Claude's summary, and where the two could differ, Jon's later statements win.

## It began as a question, not as an app

In July 2026 Jon was using Spotify's AI playlist feature to walk himself through music deliberately, one prompt at a time. He described his own path as a "character arc": pop, R&B, rap, EDM, indie, folk, lofi, Japanese music, K-pop, Latin, Portuguese, jazz. He asked for the next arcs and for the gaps, and found them: whole regions he had never heard, and almost nothing before 1950. Three things from that summer carried into everything after:

- He wants the real thing, not the export version. Of Japan he asked for "the second face of Japan", the music outside Tokyo and Osaka that does not perform for outsiders.
- New sound is tiring. After a run of unfamiliar music he asked for "something closer to home", "a break from the ear fatigue of new sound". Exploration needs a rhythm of going out and coming back.
- A playlist prompt is a blunt instrument for a precise feeling. Getting an exact emotional register out of Spotify's AI took many failed attempts; naming songs and getting yes or no worked better.

On 5 November 2026 he came back with a thesis to test: that genres change with the medium music is heard through, the listening devices and places, more than with "the vibe of the era". The conversation that followed set the tone of the whole project. He would not accept a study's conclusion without asking what it had measured ("how is it measured, what are they really even measuring, and do the words match"), would not accept "a billion users" as evidence of influence, and stopped Claude when it began agreeing with him to be agreeable. He wanted it shown, not argued.

## From a chart to a family tree

To look at the thesis, Claude drew a chart: genres as bars across time, with the arrival of each listening technology marked. Jon's corrections to that chart are the first design decisions of the app.

- **A genre is a living thing.** He could not find rock alive in 2026 on the chart, though "three or four radio stations in the city" still played it and the people he had worked with in a warehouse listened to little else. "If rock music was a living, breathing entity, where has it travelled all these years, in and out of different subgenres." A genre needs stages: when it first existed, when it took hold, its peak, when it left the charts, when it survives as a niche with its own stations, and, rarely, when nobody plays it at all.
- **Songs are there to give the feel of an era.** "The purpose of having the songs is mostly for me to get a vibe of the era." The most popular and most historically relevant come first; the historical firsts that nobody knows are an introduction, "context rather than the main body".
- **It should cover the world and most of recorded history.** "Make for me the world map so I can spatially see the regions or specific places where music originated from. But in order to do that you need a more complete family tree of genres, this time worldwide and for most of recorded human history", drawn loosely, "like is done for programming languages or for natural languages".
- **A tradition is continuous, not a point.** "You can't tell me Chinese ritual and court music stayed the same for 2000 years until Chinese opera. Country in 1925 and 2020 are kind of different and kind of the same. So I think we can't just represent things as nodes." That is why the data has long-lived traditions with movements inside them.
- **Zooming must not change the picture.** Important lines are thick from far away and the rest pencil-thin; as you zoom in the names appear on top of the same shape.
- **It will take many passes.** "You will likely not get it your first try with something so complicated, so do your best."

That became the first product, the World Music Family Tree: a tree, a map, a list with a definition and a Spotify prompt for every movement. Its purpose, in Jon's words from the notes: to explore the world history of music bit by bit through one playlist per genre, with varied moods but not too long on obscure material, the most significant first, moving home, abroad, back in time, and home again, so the ears do not tire.

## The time machine

The second product turned the same data around. Instead of following a genre through time, stand in a place at a moment and hear what was playing. Jon's statement of the goal is the test the project is still held to:

> "I want any playlist selected to impress a local or an old person from any location. I want them to feel nostalgia to the point they eventually are compelled to comment. If it is not the case, then what the time machine shows is a fake version of the past."

What followed from it:

- **Local first, like a local radio.** By default a playlist leans to the music made there, not to the foreign hits that also charted.
- **Tested on real people.** He tried a Swedish playlist for the 1950s and 60s on his grandparents, whose era and country it was.
- **Hits need evidence.** Genre examples were not enough, so research began into year-by-year lists from published charts, each song with its source, checked by a blind synthetic listener and then by Jon. A list had to score well, be mostly real hits of that year, contain nothing doubtful, and cover every scene the listener named. Places that fell short were marked sparse and shown as sparse, not dressed up.
- **Research follows demand.** New country-decades before repairs to weak ones, and what people ask for, copy, or visit decides the order.
- **The prompt is an invitation, not an order.** Seed songs are a starting point, not a list to reproduce; the wording should be natural, not mechanical; the playlist should run long.
- **A song keeps its own name.** Spotify accepts Japanese and Korean script, so the original and the romanised form are both kept for each track, separate from the language the prompt is written in.

Jon also began weighing whether this could grow beyond a personal tool. His view then: the valuable thing is the data, a real record of what was a hit in each country in each year. He decided against keeping cover images inside the project because it would balloon, and wanted a feature only once it changed the result.

## From a private page to a product

The app lived as a page inside Claude that Jon shared by link. Two people he shared it with left no trace, because visitors to such a page cannot save anything. That, and wanting real users, feedback and numbers, led to the decision on 22 November 2026 to build a minimum product on real hosting. His terms, briefly: anyone may use it without an account; a profile is by request and by endorsement from existing users, with a ceiling; testers raise and vote on issues, and he approves what gets worked on; sign-in stronger than a PIN; a profile holds the minimum and points at things by reference; separate development and live versions; nothing reaches users or their data without his approval; no possibility of a surprise bill; open source and open to contributions. And the look: "a clean iOS style feel, minimal aesthetic", "like a phone app or commercially viable mobile site", after repeated versions that felt generic.

## What the app is, as Claude understands it

It is three things that share one body of data.

1. **A time machine.** Stand in a place and a time and hear it truthfully. Judged by whether someone who was there recognises it.
2. **A map of how music is related.** The tree, the globe and the tunnel show where sounds came from, what they became, and which are alive. Judged by whether someone can see their own music in it and follow it somewhere new.
3. **A growing record of music itself.** Songs, artists, places and years with their sources. Judged by whether the things a person knows are in it, and by whether it can keep giving them something they have not heard.

One person's way of listening runs through all three: go out to something unfamiliar, come home, go further next time. The app exists to make that journey easy for other people, and to be honest with them at each step about what is known and what is thin.

## What has stayed constant

- Truth to lived experience is the measure. Popularity statistics, tree diagrams and scores are means to it.
- Words must match what was measured. A number is examined before it is used.
- Thin or uncertain data is labelled, never disguised.
- Familiar ground and new ground alternate.
- The work is iterative, and a first attempt is expected to be wrong in places.
- Jon wants to be shown evidence and options, not agreed with.

## Outside the app

One goal belongs to how Jon works, not to what the app is: developing from his own laptop with Claude Code or a similar tool, and loading the in-progress version locally. He has said this is a separate conversation to have later, part of his workflow and not of the product's design. It is recorded here so it is not mistaken for an app feature or forgotten.
